# Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia and Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (BPH/LUTS)

## Definition

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a histological diagnosis characterized by non-malignant proliferation of prostatic stromal and epithelial cells in the transition zone, leading to prostatic enlargement. Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) encompass the storage, voiding, and post-micturition symptoms that commonly result from BPH-related bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), though LUTS may also arise from other etiologies including overactive bladder (OAB), nocturnal polyuria, or detrusor underactivity.

## Pathophysiology

Prostatic enlargement in BPH involves two pathophysiologic components:

**Static component**: Physical compression of the prostatic urethra by hyperplastic nodules in the transition zone, creating mechanical obstruction. Prostate volume correlates with symptom severity and disease progression risk.

**Dynamic component**: Increased smooth muscle tone in the prostatic stroma, bladder neck, and prostatic capsule mediated by alpha-1 adrenergic receptors (predominantly alpha-1A subtype). This accounts for approximately 40% of urethral resistance.

**Secondary bladder changes**: Chronic BOO leads to compensatory detrusor hypertrophy, trabeculation, and eventually decompensation with detrusor underactivity, elevated post-void residual, and overflow incontinence. Detrusor overactivity develops in approximately 50-60% of men with BOO.

Hormonal factors include age-related increases in estrogen-to-androgen ratio and intraprostatic DHT accumulation despite declining serum testosterone levels. Growth factors (FGF, EGF, TGF-beta) and chronic prostatic inflammation contribute to tissue remodeling.

## Classification

### Symptom Severity by IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score)
- **Mild**: IPSS 0-7
- **Moderate**: IPSS 8-19
- **Severe**: IPSS 20-35
- Quality of life (QoL) question scored separately (0-6), with >=4 indicating significant bother

### Symptom Categories
**Storage (irritative) symptoms**: Frequency, urgency, nocturia, urgency incontinence
**Voiding (obstructive) symptoms**: Weak stream, hesitancy, intermittency, straining, prolonged voiding time
**Post-micturition symptoms**: Incomplete emptying, post-void dribbling

### Uroflowmetry Parameters
- **Normal**: Qmax >15 mL/s (voided volume >=150 mL required for valid study)
- **Equivocal**: Qmax 10-15 mL/s
- **Obstructed**: Qmax <10 mL/s
- Flow pattern: bell-shaped (normal) vs. plateau (obstruction) vs. intermittent (straining/detrusor underactivity)

## Etiology

- Age: prevalence increases from ~25% at age 40-49 to ~80% at age 70-79 (histologic BPH)
- Androgens: functional testes and DHT are required for BPH development
- Metabolic syndrome: obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia are associated with BPH progression
- Family history: first-degree relatives have increased risk
- Race: higher clinical prevalence in Black men
- Chronic prostatic inflammation

## Clinical Features

**Symptoms**: Progressive voiding difficulty, weak urinary stream, hesitancy, nocturia (often most bothersome), urinary frequency, urgency, sensation of incomplete emptying, terminal dribbling.

**Signs**: Enlarged prostate on DRE (smooth, firm, elastic), distended bladder (if retention present).

**Complications if untreated**: Acute urinary retention (AUR), recurrent urinary tract infections, bladder stones, gross hematuria, obstructive uropathy with renal insufficiency (bilateral hydroureteronephrosis), overflow incontinence, detrusor failure.

## Diagnosis

- **History and IPSS**: Quantify symptom severity and impact on quality of life
- **Digital rectal examination (DRE)**: Estimate prostate size, assess for nodules suspicious for malignancy
- **PSA**: Serves as a surrogate for prostate volume; PSA >1.4 ng/mL predicts progression (MTOPS, REDUCE data); also screens for concurrent prostate cancer
- **Urinalysis**: Exclude UTI, hematuria, glycosuria
- **Serum creatinine**: If renal obstruction is suspected
- **Uroflowmetry**: Non-invasive assessment of voiding pattern and Qmax
- **Post-void residual (PVR)**: Ultrasound or catheterization; PVR >100-200 mL suggests significant retention
- **Transrectal or transabdominal ultrasound**: Prostate volume measurement (guides 5ARI use if >40 mL and surgical planning)
- **Pressure-flow study (urodynamics)**: When diagnosis is uncertain, failed medical therapy, or prior to invasive treatment in select patients
- **Cystoscopy**: Not routine; indicated if hematuria, suspicion of stricture, or pre-operative assessment

## Treatment

### Watchful Waiting / Behavioral Therapy
- Appropriate for mild symptoms (IPSS <8) with minimal bother
- Fluid management, timed voiding, avoidance of caffeine and alcohol before bedtime, review of medications (anticholinergics, diuretics, decongestants)

### Medical Therapy

**Alpha-1 adrenergic blockers** (first-line for moderate-severe LUTS):
- Tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, silodosin 8 mg daily (uroselective alpha-1A)
- Alfuzosin 10 mg XR daily
- Doxazosin 4-8 mg daily, terazosin 5-10 mg daily (less selective, more hypotension)
- Onset: symptom improvement within 2-4 weeks

**5-alpha reductase inhibitors (5ARI)** (for prostate volume >40 mL):
- Finasteride 5 mg daily (type II 5AR inhibitor)
- Dutasteride 0.5 mg daily (dual type I and II inhibitor)
- Reduce prostate volume by 20-30% over 6-12 months; reduce AUR and surgery risk by ~50%
- Side effects: decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, ejaculatory disorders, gynecomastia

**Combination therapy** (alpha-blocker + 5ARI):
- Superior to monotherapy for large prostates with moderate-severe LUTS
- MTOPS trial: 66% reduction in progression risk vs. placebo
- CombAT trial: superior to monotherapy for prostate >30 mL after 2 years

**PDE5 inhibitors**: Tadalafil 5 mg daily (FDA-approved for BPH with or without ED; improves IPSS by ~2-4 points)

**Beta-3 agonists**: Mirabegron 25-50 mg or vibegron 75 mg daily (for predominant storage symptoms/OAB component, may combine with alpha-blocker)

**Anticholinergics**: Cautious use for storage-predominant symptoms if PVR is low (<150 mL); risk of retention in BOO

### Surgical and Minimally Invasive Therapy

**Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP)**: Gold standard for prostate 30-80 mL; bipolar preferred (lower TUR syndrome risk). IPSS improvement ~70%, Qmax improvement 120-150%.

**Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP)**: Size-independent; particularly advantageous for prostates >80 mL; outcomes comparable to open prostatectomy with less morbidity. Steeper learning curve.

**Photoselective vaporization (PVP/GreenLight)**: Good for patients on anticoagulation; for prostates 30-80 mL.

**Aquablation (AquaBeam)**: Robotic waterjet ablation guided by real-time ultrasound; preserves ejaculatory function better than TURP (WATER and WATER II trials); for prostates 30-150 mL.

**Prostatic urethral lift (UroLift)**: Mechanical retraction of lateral lobes; preserves sexual function; for prostates 30-80 mL without median lobe. Modest symptom improvement.

**Water vapor thermal therapy (Rezum)**: Convective radiofrequency water vapor injections; office-based; preserves sexual function; for prostates 30-80 mL including median lobe.

**Simple prostatectomy (open, laparoscopic, or robotic)**: For very large prostates (>80-100 mL) or concurrent bladder pathology (large diverticulum, stones).

## Prognosis

- BPH is a progressive condition: MTOPS data showed ~17% progression rate over 4 years on placebo
- Risk factors for progression: prostate volume >30 mL, PSA >1.4 ng/mL, age, severity of symptoms, low Qmax
- Medical therapy significantly reduces progression risk and need for surgery
- Surgical interventions provide durable symptom relief; TURP remains effective at 10-15 year follow-up
- Quality of life: successful treatment improves IPSS, Qmax, and patient-reported QoL scores substantially
